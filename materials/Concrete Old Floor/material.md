# Concrete Old Floor

Create a seamless procedural material matching the supplied references: an old concrete floor with a heavily worn dark surface layer, broad overlapping abrasion marks, and irregular exposure of the warm sandy substrate beneath. The result should feel industrial, compacted, and aged by years of traffic and rough cleaning rather than freshly poured or uniformly stained.

## Material identity

The floor is dominated by a dark charcoal to desaturated blue-gray coating or grime layer over tan-gray concrete. Wear cuts through the dark layer in sweeping, broken gestures, revealing a lighter beige mineral body in irregular islands, thin streaks, and granular speckling. The surface remains visually continuous and mostly flat; its variation comes from accumulated abrasion, embedded grit, and shallow damage rather than separate slabs or deep structural cracking.

## Color and pattern

- Center the dark layer on charcoal gray, soot black, muted slate blue, and subdued olive-gray.
- Use dusty beige, warm taupe, and pale brown-gray for the exposed concrete beneath.
- Organize the large-scale pattern into overlapping curved sweeps, scuffed arcs, dragged streaks, and broad cloudy wear fields.
- Break the edges of exposed areas into dense granular flecks and ragged, chipped contours instead of clean graphic boundaries.
- Scatter tiny warm and cool aggregate specks through both layers, with only very rare muted rust-red or mineral-colored points.
- Allow a few thin scratches and dragged lines to cross the broader wear pattern, but keep them secondary and irregular.
- Maintain a dark-over-light balance in which neither layer becomes a simple background with isolated spots.
- Avoid slab grids, repeated circular swirls, camouflage-like blobs, bright paint colors, sharp vector shapes, and uniformly distributed noise.

## Surface response

The floor is dry, non-metallic, and predominantly rough. The dark worn coating can be marginally smoother or denser than the exposed concrete, while the beige abraded areas should feel dusty and porous. Keep reflections broad and weak, with subtle roughness variation following the wear pattern. Avoid wet gloss, oily highlights, polished stone reflections, and metallic sparkle.

## Relief and normals

Relief should be shallow and tightly controlled. Give the concrete a continuous fine gritty texture with subtle pitting and compressed aggregate. Let exposed beige regions sit only slightly below the surviving dark layer, using feathered chipped edges and fine granular breakup. Add restrained abrasion grooves, scuffs, and occasional narrow scratches that follow varied sweeping directions. Avoid deep cracks, raised paint flakes, heavy displacement, sharp trenches, and relief strong enough to make the surface resemble bark or eroded rock.

## Scale and tiling

Preserve three scales of variation: broad interlocking dark and exposed fields, medium sweeping abrasion gestures, and dense fine mineral grain. The largest marks should cross and dissolve into one another rather than forming isolated recognizable symbols. The material must tile seamlessly without repeating a distinctive swirl, scratch, exposed island, or bright aggregate fragment at 1x, 2x, and 4x repetition.

## Acceptance targets

- Reads immediately as an old, heavily abraded concrete floor with remnants of a dark surface layer.
- Balances charcoal and blue-gray coating with irregular warm beige substrate exposure.
- Features broad overlapping sweep marks, ragged wear boundaries, and dense granular breakup.
- Remains mostly flat, dry, rough, and non-metallic with restrained shallow relief.
- Tiles seamlessly at 1x, 2x, and 4x without obvious recurring swirls or exposed patches.
- Avoids fresh concrete, asphalt, natural stone, painted camouflage, deep cracking, and glossy oil-stained flooring.
