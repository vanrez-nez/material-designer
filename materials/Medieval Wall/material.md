# Medieval Wall

Create a seamless procedural material matching the supplied references: an old lime-plastered wall with a chalky ivory surface, warm sandy underlayers, broad worn patches, ragged erosion, shallow fissures, and dirt caught in recessed texture. The wall should feel hand-applied, repaired, and weathered over a long period without exposing a regular brick or stone pattern.

## Material identity

The surface is predominantly pale cream, bone, and warm off-white lime plaster. Irregular areas have thinned, abraded, or flaked away to reveal beige, ochre, and light brown substrate. Layer boundaries are soft in broad areas but become broken and jagged around local losses. Overlapping trowel smears, compressed patches, shallow cracks, small cavities, and dark grime create an uneven historic finish.

## Color and pattern

- Center the palette on chalky ivory, warm white, pale cream, and light parchment beige.
- Reveal muted sand, tan, ochre, and honey-brown underlayers through irregular worn patches.
- Add restrained gray-beige shadows and dark brown grime along recesses, fissures, and damaged edges.
- Build the wear in nested scales: broad faded regions, medium islands of exposed undercoat, ragged flake boundaries, fine scratches, and tiny pits.
- Include occasional warmer stains and thin darker streaks, keeping them sparse and naturally embedded.
- Let pale plaster overlap and partially cover older layers so the surface reads as accumulated application and erosion rather than a single mottled coat.
- Avoid regular masonry joints, repeated stone shapes, bright clean white, strong orange staining, uniform noise, and obvious procedural cells.

## Surface response

The finish is dry, chalky, and highly matte. Roughness should remain high overall, with slight variation between smoother compressed plaster, powdery pale deposits, exposed sandy undercoat, and dirtier cavities. The material is non-metallic and should scatter light softly without glossy, wet, polished, or waxed highlights.

## Relief and normals

Relief is layered and moderately pronounced while remaining appropriate to a thin wall coating. Use broad low-frequency undulation for uneven hand application, medium raised plaster islands and eroded recesses, ragged chipped boundaries, shallow branching fissures, trowel drag marks, and fine granular pores. The largest damage should feel like missing plaster layers rather than broken rock. Avoid deep masonry gaps, sharp cliff-like displacement, thick curling paint flakes, or uniformly rough noise.

## Scale and tiling

Several broad intact and worn plaster regions should cross the visible object, with many medium erosion islands and fine surface marks nested within them. The wall should read as pale historic plaster at mid distance while revealing layered wear and grime up close. Every channel must tile seamlessly without border discontinuities, mirrored seams, repeated damage clusters, obvious horizontal courses, or a dominant centered patch.

## Acceptance targets

- Immediately reads as an aged medieval lime-plaster wall.
- Preserves the references' ivory surface, sandy exposed layers, and warm weathered palette.
- Shows broad worn areas with ragged chipped boundaries, shallow cracks, and accumulated grime.
- Has a dry high-roughness response with layered but believable plaster relief.
- Contains no regular brick, block, or stone-joint pattern.
- Remains non-metallic, seamless, and convincing at 1x, 2x, and 4x tiling.
- Does not drift toward marble, natural stone, concrete, clean modern stucco, peeling paint, or heavily ruined masonry.
